upwind

How it works

The air around you arrived from somewhere. Upwind takes the place you choose, starts 500 metres above it, and steps backwards through the wind, hour by hour, for up to ten days. The line on the globe is the path that air took to reach you.

Where the wind comes from

The wind is from the Global Forecast System, the weather model run by the United States National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) four times a day. NOAA publishes it freely. Upwind keeps the last ten days: wind speed, wind direction and rising or sinking motion on a one-degree grid at seventeen heights, every three hours.

How the path is found

At each step the engine reads the wind where the air is, moves the air against it to a first guess of where it was a few minutes earlier, reads the wind there, and moves again with the average of the two. This is the same scheme NOAA's own trajectory model, HYSPLIT, uses. Air rises and sinks as well as drifting sideways, so the path is followed in three dimensions; the line is drawn above the globe, with heights exaggerated so you can see them, over a faint track on the ground.

The engine is written in Rust and runs in your browser. Your device downloads only the wind along the path, a few megabytes, and computes the path itself.

How far to trust it

A path like this is an estimate, and it gets less certain the further back it goes. Small differences in where and how high the air starts can send it somewhere quite different a week earlier. That is why Upwind draws several fainter paths around the main one: they start a little to each side and at other heights. Where they stay together, the answer is solid. Where they fan out, read the far end as "roughly from that direction".

We compared Upwind with HYSPLIT for the same starting points and times. Against HYSPLIT run on wind of the same resolution, the paths differ by about a quarter of the distance travelled, which is the usual difference between two trajectory models. Near high mountains the height of the path is less reliable. The wind near the ground is also more turbulent than any model at this scale can show, so the air at street level is a mixture: this is the path of the air mass, and not of each molecule in it.

What leaves your device

  • When you type a place, the text you type goes to the Open-Meteo place search, which returns matching places and their coordinates.
  • When you choose "Use my location", your browser gives the page your position. The page rounds it to about a kilometre and uses it on your device. It appears in the page address so you can share the result; nothing else stores it.
  • The page downloads wind files for the regions the path crosses. They are stored at GitHub, apart from the page itself, so GitHub receives those requests. Which files are requested shows roughly where the path went.
  • When you first open the page, it looks up the city your device's time zone is named after, to have something to show.

Sources and thanks
